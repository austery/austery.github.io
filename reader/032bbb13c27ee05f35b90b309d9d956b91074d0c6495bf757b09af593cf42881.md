### 睡眠与真正恢复的区别
你每年睡眠接近 3000 个小时。那为什么醒来时却依然感到筋疲力尽？

因为睡眠、休息与恢复是完全不同的三件事。如果你雄心勃勃，“多休息”听起来就像是在“掉队”。于是你咬牙硬撑着疲惫，一路走到精疲力竭。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
You sleep nearly 3,000 hours a year. So why do you keep waking up exhausted? Because sleep and rest and recovery are three completely different things. If you're ambitious, rest more sounds like fall behind. So you push through the fatigue all the way to burnout.
</details>

但高效能人士并不是休息得更多，而是休息得更不同。从我担任 CEO 的岁月到坐在估值数十亿美元的董事会会议室里，我深刻体会到了一个道理：我如何恢复，就是我最大的优势。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
But the high performers don't rest more. They rest differently. From my CEO days to sitting in billion dollar boardrooms, I learned one thing the hard way. That is how I recover is my biggest advantage.
</details>

因此在这个视频播客中，我将向你展示顶尖水平的恢复究竟是如何运作的。一个三步恢复系统，以及一件任何睡眠或假期都无法为你做到的事。我们现在开始。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
So in this video podcast, I will show you how elite recovery actually works. A three-step recovery system and that one thing that no sleep or vacation can do for you. Let's get going.
</details>

### 恢复悖论与积极休整
你称之为休息的大多数行为，其实都是“深度伪休息”。它看起来像那么回事，但其实不是，因为放松与恢复在大脑中的感受可能截然不同。

心理学家萨宾·索嫩塔格（Sabine Sonnentag）曾在德国开展研究，她花了多年时间研究“恢复悖论”：你的工作压力越大，从中恢复过来就越困难。你可能正在散步，但在脑海中却在和一个根本不在场的人争吵。压力并没有离开。关键在于要明白，任何深度休息都必须切实改变你的心智状态。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Most of what you call rest is deep fake rest. It looks like the real thing, but it's not because relaxing and recovery can feel very different to your brain. Sabine Sonnentag is a psychologist. She used to be in Germany and she spent years studying the recovery paradox. The more stressful your work becomes, the harder it is to recover from it. You might be taking a walk, but arguing with someone in your head who isn't there. The stress doesn't go away. The key then is to understand that any deep rest must actually change your state of mind.
</details>

研究人员发现的第二件更令人惊讶的事情是：当你尝试在某件事上变得更精通时——比如钢琴、吉他、绘画、学跳舞、烹饪等等——这些体验会在第二天早晨为你带来更充沛的精力和更高的投入度。有时它们比实际的身体休息更能让你放松和恢复。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Now the second more surprising thing that researchers have discovered is that when you try to get better at something, like piano or guitar, painting, learning to dance, cooking, whatever, those experiences bring greater energy and engagement to your next morning. Sometimes they relax you and restore you more than the actual physical rest.
</details>

年轻时我也有过非常相似的经历。那时我正在接受僧侣修行的训练，但与人们想象中只是静静坐着冥想数小时不同，我的每一天都被琐事和体力劳作排得满满当当：做饭、洗衣服、打扫厕所、清扫庭院，而在一天结束时，我却感到彻底恢复了精力。我的老师曾经说过这样一句话：你会在无私的奉献中找到内心的平静。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
When I was younger, I had a very similar experience. I was training to become a monk, but unlike the idea of monkhood where you just sit in silence and meditate for hours and hours, my days were completely jam-packed with chores and physical services, cooking, washing, cleaning toilets, cleaning the courtyard, and I felt so restored by the end of the day. My teacher used to say this, you'll find stillness in selfless service.
</details>

所以关于休息和恢复，有两个非常违反直觉的观念：
- 第一，休息绝不仅仅是减少消耗和无所事事；
- 第二，做你热爱的事情可以让你感觉像是在休息。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
So these are the two very counterintuitive ideas about rest and restoration. First, rest isn't simply about reducing effort and doing nothing. And second, work that you love can feel like rest.
</details>

### “紧绷与恢复”评估工具
我使用一个耗时 20 秒的工具，称之为“紧绷与收益”（Grip and Gain）。在接下来的三到四天里，在下班后、休息后，给自己打两个从 1 到 5 的分数。

“紧绷”（Grip）：工作还在你脑海中占据多少分量？你抓得有多紧？这就是你的紧绷度。1 分意味着你可以完全放下甩开，5 分意味着你根本无法停止去想它。

而在另一边是“收益”（Gain）：这个刻度关乎休息后你感觉好转了多少。5 分意味着显著恢复，1 分意味着你依然焦躁不安。

低紧绷、高收益，这就是你的深度休息。高紧绷、低收益，那就是深度伪休息。一天中有三个时刻你可以降低紧绷并提高收益，我把它们称为“3R”。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
I use a 20-second tool that I call grip and gain. For the next three or four days, rate yourself after work, after you rest, two scores from one to five. Grip how much work is still on your mind. How tight are you holding on to it? That's your grip. One means you can shrug it off. Five means you can't switch it off. And on the other side is the gain. Now that dial is about how much better do you feel after rest? A score of five means significantly restored. One means you're still restless. Low grip, high gain, that's your deep rest. High grip, low gain, that's deep fake rest. There are three moments in your day when you can lower your grip and raise your gain. I call them the three Rs.
</details>

### 第一个 R：再充电（Recharge）
我们从第一个 R 开始：再充电（Recharge）。顶尖运动员不会等到周末才恢复，他们把恢复融入到训练之中。顺便说一下，我想请大家帮个小忙，很多观众每周都会回到这个频道，但其实并没有真正订阅。如果你是其中之一，请花两秒钟订阅一下。这样你能继续成长并打造精彩的生活，我们也会继续为你制作高质量的深度内容。非常感谢。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Let's start with the first R, recharge. Top athletes don't wait for the weekend to recover. They build recovery into their training. And by the way, a quick favor I'd love to ask, a lot of viewers come back to this channel every week, but they haven't actually subscribed. So if you are one of them, please take two seconds and please subscribe. That way, you keep building and growing your amazing life and we'll keep building high quality in-depth content for you. Thank you for doing that.
</details>

这里有一个令人惊讶的发现：如果你喝了咖啡然后再小睡 15 分钟，你的感觉会比单喝咖啡更好。这是我研究这个话题时学到的最神奇的事情。拉夫堡大学（Loughborough University）的睡眠研究人员在下午容易困倦的时段让昏昏欲睡的驾驶员进入模拟器。那些喝了咖啡因的人，驾驶事故减少了 66%。这很好，咖啡因确实有帮助。但那些摄入了咖啡因然后小睡 15 分钟的人，事故发生率降低了 91%。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Here's a surprise. If you drink coffee then take a 15-minute nap, you'll feel better than drinking coffee alone. This is the strangest thing I learned while researching for this topic. Sleep researchers at Loughborough University put sleepy drivers in a simulator during the afternoon slump, right? Those who drank caffeine cut driving incidents by 66%. So, great, caffeine helps. But those who took caffeine and then took a 15-minute nap cut them down by 91%.
</details>

我们大多数人会想：“噢不行，我没有时间小睡。”但温斯顿·丘吉尔（Winston Churchill）在二战期间领导英国时，每天下午都有时间小睡。你总能找到时间的。

现在，有时这些策略确实很难执行，但恰恰在你最繁忙的时候，它们才最为关键。营销发布在即、交易必须敲定、家里添了新生儿……在那些根本没有时间进行真正休假的日子里，你的精力收益必须在几分钟内达成。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
And most of us would think, oh no, I I don't have time to nap. But Winston Churchill had time to take a nap every afternoon while he was leading the United Kingdom through the Second World War. You can always find time. Now, sometimes these tactics are hard to implement, but they matter the most exactly when you're the busiest. A marketing launch is coming, a deal has to be closed, a newborn at home, days when there's no time for a real break, when your gain has to come in minutes.
</details>

因此，我常练习两种短时恢复技巧：
- 第一种是午餐后进行 **15 分钟的 NSDR**。NSDR 代表非睡眠深度休息（non-sleep deep rest），即瑜伽睡眠（yoga nidra）。如果你在办公室，可以躺下或舒适地坐着，闭上双眼，播放任何免费的瑜伽睡眠或 NSDR 音频。YouTube 上有很多，挑选你喜欢的，让引导语带领你放松身体。
- 如果你连 15 分钟都没有，遇上了极其繁忙的日子，那就找出至少 5 分钟来做所谓的**循环叹气（cyclic sighing）**：深吸一口舒适的长气，然后追加吸入短短的第二口气，接着通过嘴巴进行长而缓慢的呼气，如果你想的话可以叹出声来。动作放轻柔，持续做两到五分钟，这会改变你的心智状态。

这就是你如何通过再充电来度过一天——利用小睡、NSDR 或者循环叹气。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
So, there are two short recovery techniques I practice. The first one is 15 minutes of NSDR after lunch. NSDR stands for non-sleep deep rest, yoga nidra. So, you can lie down or sit comfortably if you're in an office, close your eyes, and you can play any free yoga nidra or NSDR track. YouTube has plenty of them. Pick the one you like and let that voice guide you through relaxing your body. Now, what if you can't even have 15 minutes? It's just one of those days, then find at least five minutes to do what is known as cyclic sighing. Take a comfortable inhale, a deep one. Then take a small second one and do a long slow exhale through the mouth and you can sigh if you want. Do it gently and do it at least for two to five minutes and that will change your state of mind. So that's how you recharge to get through the day, either an app or NSDR or that cyclic sigh.
</details>

### 第二个 R：任务归宿（Return）
更难的部分出现在工作结束之后。你如何给工作一个“寄回地址”，让它不再追着你不放？你必须把它打发走。休息中最难的部分莫过于此：你的工作应当停止纠缠你。

卡尔·纽波特（Cal Newport）写过一本很棒的书叫《深度工作》（Deep Work），不是深度休息，是深度工作。当他在麻省理工学院（MIT）攻读博士学位时，压力非常大，MIT 就是会让人那样。但他每天都会以同样的方式收尾：更新任务清单，给工作设定一个回执归宿。我们马上会细讲这点。他会检查接下来几天的计划，然后合上笔记本电脑，大声说一句：“日程关闭完毕。”听起来有时挺荒唐的，但他的逻辑是：一旦每项任务都有了规划，大脑就不必再去时刻惦记着它。即使他现在成了畅销书作家和教授，他依然保持着同样的仪式。

所以你看，当工作完成时，确实有办法把它们彻底关掉。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Now the harder part comes when your work has wrapped up. How do you give your work a return address so it stops chasing you? You have to send it away. The hardest part of rest is this. Your work should stop following you. Cal Newport wrote a great book called Deep Work, not Deep Rest, Deep Work. But when he was a PhD student at MIT, he was super stressed. MIT does that to you. He would end every day the same way. He would update his task list, he would give it a return address. We'll talk about that. He would check his plan for the coming days, then he would close his laptop and say out loud, schedule shutdown complete. Sounds ridiculous sometimes, but his reasoning was that once every task had a plan, his mind no longer had to keep track of it. He still runs the same ritual even though he's now a best-selling author and a professor. So yeah, there is a way to turn things off when you're done working with them.
</details>

苏珊·沃西基（Susan Wojcicki）也是如此。她在 2014 年成为 YouTube 的 CEO，执掌了 9 年，帮助它成长为月活 20 亿用户的平台。但她要求自己每天下午 6 点前回家与家人共进晚餐。她告诉团队在 6 点到 9 点之间不要联系她。等孩子们睡下后，她会再次查看邮件。她曾经说过：“界限能让你成为更好的领导者。”我很喜欢这句话。我想她在几年前去世了，当时大约五十多岁，多么巨大的损失。

但工作对所有人来说都可能是一个永无休止的“开放循环”，你的大脑会一直抓着不放。我认识不少人爬到了顶峰，却彻底精疲力竭，从不花时间和朋友或爱人相处。我总在想：如果你站在顶峰时孑然一身，那没有任何成功值得你去攀登。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
And the same story with Susan Wojcicki, who became the CEO of YouTube in 2014 and she ran it for nine years and helped it grow to 2 billion users a month. But she aimed to be home with her family by 6:00 p.m. for dinner every day. She told her team not to contact her between six and nine. And after her kids went to bed, she would check her email again. She used to say, limits make you a better leader. I love that line. She passed away a couple of years ago, I think, in her mid 50s. What a loss. But work can be a never-ending open loop for all of us. Your brain can keep holding onto it. I know enough people who got to the top fully burnt out and never spend time with their friends or their loved ones. And I always think, no success is worth climbing toward if you're standing at the top all alone.
</details>

好，核心工具来了：**给每个未完成的任务一个“归宿地址”**。在下班前的最后 5 分钟，分为三步：
1. **第一步**：列出每一个待办行动项；
2. **第二步**：写下能推动该任务前进的最微小、最具体的行动；
3. **第三步**：将其安排进日程。

比如你可以写：明天上午 9:30，我来写营销演示文稿的第一张幻灯片。然后合上电脑，转身离开，去过属于你的其余生活——那本来就是你工作的意义所在。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Okay, so here's the key tool. Give every unfinished task a return address. In your last five minutes of work, three steps. Step one, list every open action item. Step two, write down the most smallest concrete action that can move it forward. And step three, schedule it. So you could say, tomorrow at 9:30 a.m., I'm going to write the first slide of the marketing presentation. And then close your laptop and walk away. Live the rest of your life. That's what you're working for anyway.
</details>

### 第三个 R：重拾闲暇（Reclaim）
一旦未完成的任务有了归宿，你的下班时间终于真正属于你了。随之而来的问题是：你该如何对待这些时间？你该怎样重拾它们？关于下班时间，问题在于：你在休息上所做的最糟糕的决定，往往是由那个最疲惫的自己做出的。

顺便提一句，如果你喜欢这些视频，你也会喜欢我的时事通讯。每周二发布，包含一个见解、一个工具、一个实践。我非常喜欢写它，链接在下方，完全免费。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Now, once your unfinished tasks have a return address, your off hours become finally yours. The question is then, what do you do with them? How do you reclaim them? The problem about off hours is this. Your worst decisions about rest are made by the most tired version of you. And by the way, if you like these videos, you'll also like my newsletter. It comes out every Tuesday. One insight, one tool, one practice. I love writing it. The link is down below. It's totally free.
</details>

村上春树是全世界读者最多的小说家之一，我很喜欢他的作品。他的角色总是在寻找失去的爱、身份、意义和联结，但他们很少直接谈论痛苦。你是在他们平凡的日常琐事中了解他们以及他们的痛苦的，文笔极其优美。

当村上开始写一部小说时，他决定在早晨写上五到六个小时，然后就彻底停下来。下午做点别的事情，晚上读会儿书、听听音乐——他热爱爵士乐——晚上 9 点上床睡觉，凌晨 4 点再次起床，日复一日。对他来说，这就是仪式。请注意他的夜晚缺少了什么：他在非工作时间不做任何与写作相关的决定，他重拾了那些时间。这种做法之所以奏效是有原因的：巴黎的研究人员发现，一整天在工作或家庭中高强度的用脑思考，会在大脑掌管自控力的大脑前额叶皮层（PFC）留下化学堆积物。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Haruki Murakami is one of the world's most wildly read novelists. I love his writing. His characters are always searching for lost love, identity, meaning, connection. But they rarely talk about their pain. You learn about them and their pain through their mundane day-to-day tasks. Beautiful writing. When Haruki starts writing a novel, he decides to write it for five or six hours in the morning, and then he just turns it off. He'll do something in the afternoon. He would read a little in the evening, listen to music. He loves jazz, and goes to bed by 9 p.m. Gets up at 4 a.m. again. Repeats. For him, that's the ritual. Now, notice what's missing from his evenings. He does not make any writing decisions in his off hours. He reclaims them. And there is a reason why this works. Researchers in Paris found that a full day of hard thinking at work or at home leaves a chemical buildup in the same part of your brain that runs self-control, PFC.
</details>

你可能会说：“好吧，谢谢你，但他是个著名作家，他不用像我一样每天工作 14 个小时，所以他当然能在晚上 9 点睡觉，而我做不到。”但情况是这样的：在成名之前，村上在东京经营一家小爵士酒吧。每天晚上打烊打扫完毕后，他回到家坐在厨房餐桌旁，每晚一直写到破晓前。他把这些称为他的“厨房餐桌小说”。那就是他重拾业余时间的方式：酒吧是他的工作，写作是他重拾的生活。

当然，我们之前讨论过像战争时期那样的极端状况，对吧？面对营销宣讲、产品发布或新生儿，在这些情况下，你的闲暇时间可能只有 15 分钟长。但你同样可以重拾那 15 分钟。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
And you may say, well, yeah, okay, thanks. He's a famous writer. He doesn't have to work 14 hours a day like I do. So sure, he can go to sleep at 9 p.m. I can't. But here's the thing. Before he was famous, Murakami ran a small jazz bar back in Tokyo. And every night, after closing the bar and cleaning up, he would come home, sit at his kitchen table and write into the hours before dawn every night. And he called them his kitchen table novels. That's how he reclaimed his off hours. His bar was his job. His writing was his reclaim. And sure, we talked about the wartime situation, right? The marketing presentation or a product launch or a baby. And your off hours in those kind of situations might just be 15 minutes long. But you can reclaim those 15 minutes too.
</details>

我的做法是，从以下三项活动中选择：
1. **静心（Calm）**：让你的身心平复下来的活动。散步、洗个澡，甚至裹上一条温暖的毯子喝杯热巧克力，任何对你管用的方式。
2. **创造（Create）**：需要你全神贯注的事物。弹吉他、画画、烹饪。
3. **联结（Connect）**：一个能让你放松、能让你享受共处时光的人。或者如果你的白天充斥着与人打交道，也可以选择通过独处与内省来与自己联结，这同样有效。

这就是 3R：再充电（Recharge）、归宿（Return）与重拾（Reclaim）。每天有三个时刻可以让你恢复精力。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
Here's what I do. Pick from three activities. One, calm. Something that calms your mind and body, a walk or a shower, even a warm blanket and hot chocolate, whatever works for you. Two, create. Something that takes your full attention, guitar, drawing, cooking. And three, connect. Someone you can relax with, someone you can spend time with and enjoy it. Or if your day was full of people, choose to connect with yourself through silence, introspection. That works too. So those are the three Rs, recharge, return, and reclaim. Three moments in every day where you can restore.
</details>

### 学会松开紧握的双手
但有一件事我们所有人都必须学会：如何松开执念。

多年前，我学到了彻底改变我一生的一课。当时我坐在北印度某处修道院冰冷的石地板上。那时我是个十几岁的少年，正在接受成为僧侣的训练。那是一段被自我怀疑吞噬的黑暗时期，那种怀疑紧紧地攫住了你。我已经好几天没吃东西了。当然，我没有钱、没有事业、没有身份。我带着深沉的创伤、羞耻感以及一长串的失败逃离了家庭。我彻底迷失了。

有一天我问年长的资深僧人：“为什么我依然感到这种痛苦？它什么时候才会结束？”

But there's one thing that we all have to learn, how to release the grip. Years ago, I learned something that changed my life forever. I was sitting on a cold stone floor in a monastery somewhere in North India. I was a teenager. I was training to be a monk. And it was one of those dark times when self-doubt takes over. You know, it grips you so hard. I had not eaten for days. And of course, I had no money, no career, no identity. I had run away from home carrying deep traumas and shame and a long list of failures. So I was completely lost. So one day I asked my senior monk and I said, why do I still feel this pain? When will it end?
</details>

他没有说话。他轻轻微笑，指了指院子里的一根木柱子。接着他给我讲了这个奇怪的小故事。

他说：有一个村民紧紧抱着一根就像那样的柱子，手指用力到指节发白。他大声呼喊求救：“谁来帮帮我，求求你们，这根柱子不肯放开我！”

整个村庄的人都在嘲笑他，因为柱子显然什么也没有抓住。这太荒唐了。

故事到这就结束了。随后年长的僧人便径直走开了。我当时心想：认真的吗？就这？这就是全部的故事？

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
And he didn't speak. He smiled softly and pointed to a wooden pole in the courtyard. And uh then he told me this strange little story. And he said, there was a villager who held on to a pole just like that one. So tightly that his knuckles would turn white. And he would cry out like somebody please, please help me. This pole won't let me go. And the whole village would laugh at him because of course the pole wasn't holding on to anything. It was just so silly. That was it. That was the story. Then the senior monk just walked away. And I was like seriously? That's it? That's the whole story?
</details>

但随着时间推移，我逐渐领悟到：我就是那个故事里的村民。

我们都是那个村民。我们都紧紧抱着那根柱子，然后怪罪柱子不肯放开我们。我们紧抓着工作、截止日期、恐惧、焦虑、那个没有同样爱我们的人，以及那些提醒我们自己还不够优秀的过去……紧抓着这一切。

但要知道，抓紧不放的是我们自己。你可以站在柱子旁边，在需要的时候甚至可以扶着它；但你也可以张开双手，松开它，然后转身离开。

谢谢大家，爱你们。

<details>
<summary>Original English</summary>
But over time it dawned on me that I was that villager in that story. We're all that villager. We all hold on to that pole. And then blame the pole for not letting us go. Holding on to our work, our deadlines, our fears, our anxieties, that person who didn't love us back, the stories that remind us we are not enough. All of it. But you know, we're the ones gripping. You can stand next to the pole. You can even hold it when you need to. But you can also open your hands, let go and walk away. Thank you. And I love you.
</details>
