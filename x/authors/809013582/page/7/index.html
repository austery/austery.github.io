The memory sector has been the strongest performer this year. Prices are rising almost monthly and Micron is already up 2x. So when $MU sells off right after Google drops a new AI model, people assume the worst: “CFO raised Capex. Is this the start of another overbuild?” That reaction completely misreads where we are in the cycle. Micron is not dealing with a traditional DRAM boom-bust. It’s sitting inside a multi-year, AI-driven supercycle with demand coming from every direction. Model sizes are still compounding. Inference is just getting started. AI agents and on-device intelligence are about to enter consumer platforms. Cloud providers, AI chip companies, and even government HPC programs have already locked 3–5 year budgets. This multi-source, cross-industry demand pattern has never existed in memory history. The old 2017–2018 playbook doesn’t apply. HBM is not a business you can scale by simply “deciding to expand.” The stacking process is brutally hard and capacity takes years to qualify. The industry isn’t expanding recklessly. It’s expanding reluctantly. Micron’s Capex hike is not aggressive expansion. It’s forced expansion. Customers have already pre-booked most of 2026 supply. HBM is structurally undersupplied and will remain so. If Micron doesn’t invest now, the real risk isn’t memory oversupply. It’s a bottleneck across the entire AI supply chain: GPUs underutilized, compute nodes delayed, inference costs stuck too high, and model upgrades slowed for everyone. Capex going up is not a warning sign. It’s confirmation of overwhelming demand. We’re nowhere near the point where oversupply is a realistic concern. The winners of this cycle will be the companies willing to scale within a structurally constrained industry. This is the real signal from Micron’s latest commentary.

Everyone keeps trying to size AI by asking how much it can add to global GDP. That question is already too small. Global GDP is roughly 100 trillion dollars. If AI were just a writing assistant, then yes, the ten year productivity boost would be a few trillion at most. That is the linear scenario. But AI is not stopping at language. Once it penetrates enterprise workflows, rewrites decision chains, and automates operational layers, the curve shifts. You move from a simple efficiency tool to a new coordination technology. At that point, the contribution is not three trillion or eight trillion. It becomes a decades long compounding engine. Twenty to thirty trillion is the conservative range. The upside becomes far larger once AGI, robotics, autonomous systems, and industrial automation evolve. Here is the part most people miss. Macro totals do not drive investment returns. Concentrated profit pools do. Technological revolutions never reward a thousand companies. They reward the few that become the infrastructure. The internet did it. Mobile did it. AI will be even more extreme. Nvidia, Google, and other system level players are not showing incremental advantages. They are showing lock in driven by a full paradigm shift. Entire value chains reassemble around them. This is why AI’s economic impact will almost always be underestimated. Averages hide power laws. The big money is never in the mean growth rate. It is in identifying who becomes the profit gravity well. If you pick the future center of value capture, you are not trading an AI cycle. You are positioning at the base of a multi decade uplift in the trajectory of the global economy.

If there was a real bubble, it happened in 2023 and 2024, when a single early model like GPT-3 or GPT-4 was enough to send Nvidia up tenfold and push valuations into triple-digit territory. That period was speculation driven by imagination rather than earnings. What we are seeing in 2025 is the opposite. This year has been about validation, not excess. The AI buildout is now funded by four real buyers with real cash flow, like Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta and their collective valuation sits near 26 times earnings, far below the seventy times we saw in the dot-com era. The market is also no longer treating AI as a single trade. Dispersion is everywhere. Google, which finally aligned hardware, software, and infrastructure, delivered a genuine rerating after years of underperformance and posted roughly fifty percent annual returns. In contrast, Amazon’s high multiple and slowing growth, and Meta’s strong growth but weak AI cloud monetization, left both stocks flat for most of the year. This is not what bubbles look like. This is what a rational repricing looks like when earnings momentum and monetization pathways decide who wins. Across all the downturns I’ve lived through, I have never seen a correction as orderly and disciplined as 2025. AI Capex is still expanding, and the business models are only beginning to show real commercial traction. You don’t disprove a technology cycle while the infrastructure is still being built. If anything, this phase is where high-quality names offer the best risk-reward, and these prices start to look more like a Black Friday sale than the end of a trend.
