不同的信用文化

在讲谁拥有它之前，先让我说说我和信用评分的关系。我在东欧长大，具体来说是在乌克兰，那里的信用卡文化和北美截然不同。在 90 年代和 2000 年代我还是个孩子的时候，拥有一张信用卡其实是件挺土的事。甚至背着房贷也会有点被人看不起，因为当时的观念是：先挣到现金，然后再买。如果买不起，就别当傻瓜去给银行付利息。是的，这种观念是有历史根源的。在共产主义时期，消费信贷基本上是不存在的。所以，唯一存在的借贷就是现金借贷，而且通常发生在亲友之间。而 1998 年俄罗斯的债务违约波及了大多数前苏联国家，摧毁了银行。银行被毁意味着房贷和信贷变得非常稀缺。因此在我童年时期，信贷或者“买自己负担不起的东西”这种概念才刚刚开始形成。

If you can't afford, don't be an idiot paying interest to the bank. Yes, this perception has its roots. Under communism, consumer credit was basically non-existent. 所以当我移民到加拿大时，我深深地、彻底地震惊于每个人居然都有信用卡。我甚至还记得当时自己根本分不清 credit（信用卡）和 debit（借记卡），因为在老家我从没用过这些词。长大过程中，我知道我父母有一张卡，就只有一张卡，如果那张卡里的钱用完了，卡就刷不了了——我搬到这里后在青少年时期才明白，那其实是一张借记卡。不过我扯远了。世界各地围绕信用卡的文化差异极大。哪怕在欧洲内部，德国、波兰、奥地利也主要靠借记卡生活。英国则更接近加拿大和美国。日本从未形成借贷文化，但韩国人离开借贷就活不了。那么，北美是怎么发展到人手一张信用卡的地步的？而且，仅仅因为买了一套你原本负担不起的房子，就要在接下来的 25 年里背负债务，这种想法怎么就成了我们眼中完全合理的人生决定？

So when I immigrated to Canada, I was deeply and utterly shocked how everybody had credit cards. But I digress. The culture around credit cards is vastly different across the world. Even within Europe, Germany, Poland, Austria mostly live off of debit cards. The UK is much closer to Canada and the US. Japan never adopted a culture of loans, but South Korea can't live without it. So, how did North America get to a point where everybody has a credit card? And the notion of being in debt for the next 25 years because you bought a house that you couldn't afford in the first place is something that we perceive as a perfectly reasonable life decision.

信用体系的起源

要回答这个问题，我们得回到 150 年前的美国农民。美国人对信贷的热爱可以一直追溯到 19 世纪，具体来说是农耕文化。当时硬币稀缺，所以农家在当地商店赊账是很普遍的事。大家凭握手达成协议，农民会在丰收后还款。在购买栅栏、谷仓、土地、牲畜、船只以及其他类似昂贵设备时，借贷是非常正常且被广泛接受的。随着人口越来越多、流动性越来越大，人们更可能和陌生人做生意。因此他们需要一种方法来评估谁值得信任，这催生了 1841 年在纽约成立的第一家信用报告机构。该机构保存着关于商人的纸质档案，记录他们的付款情况，但也记录他们的种族、性别和所谓的“道德品格”。那就是今天征信机构的鼻祖。

英文原文

For that we have to go back 150 years to the American farmers. The roots of American love for credit go all the way back to the 19th century and specifically to the farming culture. Coin was scarce, so it was quite common for farm families to run up debts with local stores. It was done between people on a handshake and farmers would repay after the harvest. Borrowing was very normal and acceptable in the context of expensive equipment like fences and barns and land and livestock and boats and other things of that kind. As the population became more numerous and mobile, people were more likely to do business with strangers. So they needed a way to assess who could be trusted, which led to the first credit reporting agency in 1841 in New York. The agency kept physical files on merchants with information about how they pay, but also about their race, gender, and so-called moral character. That was the ancestor of today's credit bureaus.

接着出现了分期付款计划。著名缝纫机品牌胜家（Singer）开始以分期付款的方式销售机器，最终在二战之后，信用卡变得常态化。我们在讲 Visa 和 Mastercard 的那期视频里讲过信用卡的故事，以及它们是如何开始的——当时有 6 万名加州人在从未申请过的情况下，收到了寄给他们的信用卡。加拿大也接入了同一个网络——Visa 和 Mastercard，所以它在很大程度上沿袭了美国的信用卡文化。在 2000 年代，超过 70,000（原文如此）的北美家庭至少拥有一张通用信用卡。当所有人都向陌生人、银行或机构借钱时——这意味着人与人之间没有私人交流，也没有私人关系——每个人都需要某种标签：值得信赖、不可靠、基本负责任等等。因此，在北美，信用评分变成了一种社会评级。但这种社会评级是由私营公司运营的，而且它影响的远不止贷款。那么，究竟是谁真正拥有你的信用评分，他们又是如何对你的生活拥有这么大话语权的？这套系统建立在五个环节之上。每一个环节都影响着下一个环节。

英文原文

Then came installment plans. Singer, a famous sewing machine brand, started selling its machines on installment and eventually after World War II, credit cards became normalized. We told the story about credit cards and how they started when they were mailed to 60,000 people in California who never asked for those credit cards in our Visa and Mastercard video. Canada was plugged into the same network, Visa and Mastercard. So it very much followed the credit card culture of the United States. In 2000s, more than 70,000 of North American households had at least one general purpose credit card. And when everybody borrows from strangers or banks or institutions, which means there is no personal communication and no personal relationship between people, everybody needs some kind of a label, trustworthy, unreliable, mostly responsible, etc. So in North America, the credit score became a kind of social rating. But the social rating is run by private companies and it affects a lot more than loans. So who really owns your credit score and how do they end up with this much say over your life? The system rests on five connections. Each one affects the next.

环节一：FICO 与征信机构

第一环，FICO。在美国和加拿大，预测你还款可能性的公司叫做 Fair Isaac Corporation，也被称为 FICO。FICO 是一家数据分析公司。他们以开发 FICO 评分而闻名，你们大多数人应该见过无数次了。这是一个从 300 到 850 的三位数，用来衡量消费者的信用风险。不过，FICO 自己并不能直接获取你的付款记录。他们是从三大征信机构——Equifax、Experian 和 TransUnion——那里拿到这些数据的。我们马上就会讲到它们。FICO 自己开发了一套数学公式，用来计算你偿还债务时的风险有多高。

英文原文

Connection one, FICO. In the United States and Canada, the company that predicts how likely you are to pay back is called Fair Isaac Corporation, also known as FICO. FICO is a data analytics company. They're known for developing a FICO score, which most of you have seen a million times. It is a three-digit number from 300 to 850 that measures consumer credit risk. Now, FICO itself doesn't have access to your payment history. They get it from the big credit bureaus, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. We'll get to them in a second. FICO itself developed a mathematical formula for calculating the score that shows how risky you are when it comes to repaying debt.

现在来说说征信机构。在成立后的前 30 年里，FICO 所有的事都是自己干的，收集付款数据、计算评分。但在 1989 年，它与征信机构展开了合作。为什么？因为在 89 年之前，每一家需要查询客户信用评分的企业，无论是银行还是房贷机构，都必须专门针对自己的业务去做这套计算。FICO 必须为每一家企业进行定制。他们得收集那家企业的消费者数据，专门重新计算所有东西，以迎合那家客户的需求。然后他们意识到了问题所在：这无法规模化，或者说能规模化，但工作量太大了。所以他们想把业务做广，提供一种快捷的方案，一种即插即用的方案，提供适用于全国任何企业的评分服务。而他们正是通过征信机构实现了这种即插即用。天哪，他们的这套运作方式可真有意思。

英文原文

Now, credit bureaus. For the first 30 years since it started, FICO was doing everything themselves, collecting data on payments, calculating the score. But in 1989, it partnered with credit bureaus. Why? Until ' 89, every business that needed to check credit scores of their customers, be it a bank or a mortgage lender, they had to do the calculation specifically for their business. FICO had to customize to every business. They had to gather the data of the consumers of that business, recalculate everything specifically to tailor to that customer. and they realized the problem. This doesn't scale or it scales but it's too much work. So they wanted to go broader and offer a quick option, a plug-and-play option and offer scoring that works for any business anywhere in the country. And they made that plugandplay possible through credit bureaus. And boy, do they have an interesting setup.

征信机构是从哪里获取关于你信用历史的数据的？它从所谓的“数据提供方”（furnisher）那里获取。数据提供方可以是银行、信用合作社、提供信用卡的百货商店、房贷机构、快速贷款服务公司、催收机构，凡此种种。基本上，任何能给你提供信贷的机构都算。这套生意的运作方式是这样的：征信机构从所有向其报告的数据提供方那里收集关于你的数据。数据提供方免费提供你的数据。然后征信机构调出这些数据，用 FICO 的模型跑一遍，得出评分。一旦有了评分，它们就把评分卖给任何来查询的人：贷款机构、房东、保险公司、雇主，应有尽有。

英文原文

Where does a credit bureau get data about your credit history? It gets it from someone called a furnisher. A furnisher can be a bank, a credit union, department store that offers credit cards, mortgage lenders, quick loan services, collection agencies, you name it. Basically, anyone who can give you credit. Now, the business works like this. Credit bureau collects data about you from all the furnishers who report to the credit bureau. The furnishers give your data for free. Then the credit bureau pulls that data, runs it against FICO's models, and gets the scores. Once they have the scores, they sell them back to whoever asks for it. A lender, a landlord, an insurer, an employer, you name it.

如果你在想：“好吧，但为什么数据提供方要免费给它们数据呢？首先，它们给出的是我的数据。不过说实话，在当今世界，隐私是一个非常主观的词。其次，它们为什么不卖钱呢？我的意思是，顺便赚一笔岂不是更好？”这就是为什么我说这是一个很有意思的设置。没有人付钱给它们，如果它们不报告也不会发生什么。但这套东西的运作方式几乎就像 LinkedIn 上的隐私模式。你知道在 LinkedIn 上，你可以用隐私模式看别人的资料，但如果你处于隐私模式，你也看不到谁看过你的资料。数据提供方也是同样的道理。它们向征信机构提供的数据越多，从其他人那里获得的益处就越多。这就像是一个庞大的基准数据集。等一下，还有一件事。你有没有注意到，每当我们说到征信机构，总是那三家？Equifax、Experian 和 TransUnion。为什么？

英文原文

If you're wondering, okay, but why are furnishers giving them data for free? First of all, it's my data they're giving. But let's be real, privacy is a very subjective term in today's world. Secondly, why aren't they selling it? I mean, might as well make money off of it. And this is why I call it an interesting setup. Nobody pays them and nothing will happen if they don't report. But the way this thing works is almost like a private mode on LinkedIn. You know how on LinkedIn you can look at people's profiles in private mode, but if you're in private mode, you can't see who looked at your profile either. Same thing with the furnishers. The more they feed their data to bureaus, the more they benefit from everybody else. It's like one massive data set of benchmark data. Hang on, but there's one more thing. Have you ever noticed that when we say credit bureaus, it's always the same three? Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Why?

三大征信机构的寡头垄断

征信机构市场之所以变成寡头垄断，原因在于计算机。在 60 年代征信机构的早期，曾经有成千上万家征信机构，每一家都分得自己的一块蛋糕。其中许多是地方性或专业性的机构，在计算机普及之前，大鱼和小鱼都有生存空间。当时的授信机构——银行、提供信用卡的零售商店、金融公司、信用合作社——想要向全国扩张。它们也在推进自动化，并开始以电子方式交换数据。因此，它们希望征信机构能够支持电子交换。然而，对于征信机构来说，无论它们需要存储的是当地居民的几千份档案，还是其他所有人的数百万份档案，自动化都是一笔巨大的成本。小型征信机构根本负担不起自动化。规模最大的几家率先实现了自动化，并将它们的系统与贷款机构连接起来，这样贷款机构就可以从其中任何一家调取数据。于是，计算机化缓慢但必然地将征信机构（原文为“euros”）市场变成了一个由三家巨鲸玩家主导的寡头垄断：Equifax、Experian 和 TransUnion。如今，这三家公司合计每年带来约 190 亿美元的收入。仅 TransUnion 一家就持有超过 2 亿份（原文为“$200 million files”）美国公民的档案，这大约占总人口的 60%。

英文原文

The reason the market of credit bureaus turned into an oligopoly are computers. In the early days of credit bureaus in the 60s, there were thousands of them and each one of them had its own slice of the pie. Many of them were local or specialized and there was room for both big and small fish until computers became widespread. institutions that issued credit back then. Banks, retail stores that offered credit cards, finance companies, credit unions wanted to expand across the country. They were also automating and started to exchange data electronically. So they wanted bureaus to support electronic exchange. For the bureau, however, automating was a massive cost regardless of whether they needed to store a few thousand files on local residents or millions of them on everybody else. And small bureaus simply couldn't afford automation. The largest ones automated first and linked their systems to lenders so that lenders could pull data from any of them. And slowly but surely, computerization turned the market of euros into an oligopoly with three whale players, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Today, those three bring about $19 billion a year combined. TransUnion alone holds more than $200 million files on American citizens, which is about 60% of the entire population.

所以是的，信用评分市场被严重垄断了，我不知道我们是否以及何时会看到颠覆者出现在地平线上。现在设身处地想一想，如果你是 Equifax、Experian 和 TransUnion。你知道自己基本上是不可撼动的。你们已经存在了几十年。生意一直都是老样子：收集人们的数据，用数学模型跑一遍，得出一个分数，然后卖掉。是不是有点无聊了？于是你转过身去想：“嗯，我还能玩点什么呢？等一下。为什么我们每次需要评分都要付钱给 FICO？我们干脆把他们踢出去怎么样？”事情是这样的：因为三大征信机构使用 FICO 的模型来提供信用评分，它们必须向 FICO 支付特许权使用费。于是，征信机构——可以说是三头垄断——采取行动，试图颠覆山头之王，也就是 FICO 本身。它们联合开发了一套信用评分算法，与 FICO 竞争，不再支付特许权使用费。这就是 VantageScore 在 2006 年诞生的经过。

So yes, credit score market is heavily monopolized and I don't know when and if we'll see the day when a disruptor shows up on the horizon. Now put yourself in the shoes of Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. You know you're basically untouchable. You've been around for decades. The business is all the same. You collect data on people. You run it through a mathematical model. You get a score and you sell it. Kind of gets boring, doesn't it? So, you turn around and go, "Hm, what else can I play with? Wait a second. Why are we paying FICO every time we need a score? How about we just kick them out?" Here's the thing. Because three biggest bureaus used FICO's models to provide credit score, they had to pay royalties to FICO. And so credit bureaus, the triopoly so to say, made a move to disrupt the king of the mountain, FICO itself. They developed a joint credit score algorithm to compete with FICO and stop paying royalties. And this is how Vantage score was born in 2006.

但这里有个问题。FICO 的模型基于每家征信机构的数据生成信用评分，这意味着无论一个人从三家机构中的哪一家获取信用评分，他的风险画像都是一样的。VantageScore 的构建方式则不同。每家征信机构各自编写了自己的公式，并用各自的数据池进行训练。所以每个评分只存在于那一家征信机构。因此，Equifax 的 700 分和 Experian 的 700 分意味着两件不同的事，因为它们的计算方式不同。而当贷款机构需要查看你的信用评分时，它们不会去核对每家征信机构分别怎么评价你。它们需要一个可靠的数字。这就是 VantageScore 从未真正吸引到贷款机构的原因。随后在 2006 年，FICO 以反垄断（原文为“antirust”）和商标侵权为由起诉了这三家征信机构。我觉得这一招挺好笑的，因为你看到一个垄断者以反垄断起诉另一个垄断者，而且还败诉了。

But there was a problem. FICO's models produced credit score based on the data from each bureau which meant that a person who would get that credit score would have the same risk profile no matter which of the three bureaus they got it from. Vantage score was built differently. Each of the bureaus wrote their own formula and they trained it on their own pulls of data. So each score existed only at that bureau. And so a 700 from Equifax and a 700 from Experian meant two different things because they were calculated differently. And when a lender needs to see your credit score, they're not going to check what each of the bureaus says you are. They need one reliable number. And this is the reason why Vantage Score was never really appealing to lenders. Then in 2006, FICO sued the three bureaus for antirust and trademark violations. Now, this move I find quite amusing because you've got a monopoly suing a monopoly for antitrust and it lost.

FICO 垄断地位的裂缝

但尽管败诉，FICO 并没有失去它的权力。为什么？因为贷款机构和监管机构明确要求的就是 FICO 评分。这是为什么呢？两个原因。第一，从贷款机构手中买下美国很大一部分房贷的政府支持企业——房利美（Fanny May）和房地美（Freddy Mack）——开始要求每一份房贷申请都必须有 FICO 评分。必须是 FICO 评分。如果你想把贷款卖给它们，你就得用 FICO。第二，FICO 的合同基本上对征信机构形成了一种锁定。如果 FICO 发现某家征信机构从 FICO 的竞争对手那里获取了评分，它们就会开始向那家征信机构收取七倍的费用。FICO 的掌控几十年来一直稳如磐石，直到今年秋天。那么，今年秋天到底发生了什么，让这个垄断出现了裂缝？

英文原文

But despite the loss, FICO did not lose its power. Why? Because lenders and regulators wanted specifically the FICO score. And why is that? Two reasons. One, governmentbacked companies that buy a huge share of American mortgages from lenders, Fanny May and Freddy Mack, started requiring a FICO score on every mortgage application. It had to be a FICO score. If you wanted to sell them your loans, you had to go with FICO. Second, FICO's contract basically creates a lock for the bureaus. If FICO finds out that a bureau got a score with FICO's competitor, they're going to start charging that bureau seven times more. FICO's grip has been firm as hell for decades until this fall. So, what is it that happened this fall that put a crack in the monopoly?

FICO 房贷评分的价格在 5 年内从大约 60 美分涨到了 10 美元。在 2026 年，他们把价格翻倍，给全行业增加了大约 5 亿美元的成本。现在，当你听到他们把成本翻倍时，你可能会觉得：“好吧，那现在是 20 美元了。有什么大不了的？”这无关 10 美元还是 20 美元。重要的是它对行业其他部分产生的连锁反应。FICO 想要 10 美元，但 10 美元这个价格是征信机构加价后的收费。Bill Palty，那位掌管监管房利美和房地美机构的美国政客，在 X 上发帖称，FICO 自 2020 年以来将价格提高了 1,800%，并且一直享有垄断地位。他还称征信机构像卡特尔（cartel）一样。截至 2026 年 9 月，他允许美国每一家贷款机构使用 VantageScore。但如果你仔细想想，VantageScore 正是他称之为卡特尔的那些公司的产品。很合理，对吧？随后在 9 月 28 日，他发帖表示两种评分将在同一张价目表上定价。第二天，FICO 失去了大约四分之一的市值。到目前为止，只有大约二十分之一的房贷使用 VantageScore，但这是 FICO 在 30 年来遇到的第一个真正的竞争对手。而讽刺的是，就连那个竞争对手也属于销售 FICO 评分的那三家征信机构。所以再说一次，整个信用评分市场就是一个大垄断。而这个垄断对你生活的影响，远远超出了贷款的范畴。

The price of FICO's mortgage score went from about 60 cents to $10 in 5 years. In 2026, they doubled the price and added about $500 million in industry costs. Now, when you hear that they doubled the cost, you may be like, "Okay, so it's $20 now. What's the big deal?" It's not about the $10 or $20. It's the ripple effect it has on the rest of the industry. FICO wanted $10, but the $10 price was the price that the bureaus were charging after the markup. Bill Palty, the American politician who runs the agency that oversees Fanny May and Freddy Mack, posted on X that FICO raised prices 1,800% since 2020 and had enjoyed a monopoly. He also called the bureaus cartelike. As of September 2026, he let every lender in the United States to use Vantage Score. But if you think about it, Vantage Score is a product of the companies that he called a cartel. Makes sense, right? Then on September 28th, he posted that both scores would be priced on one grid. The next day, FICO lost about a quarter of its value. So far, only about 1 in 20 mortgage loans use Vantage Score, but it is the first real competition FICO has had in 30 years. And the irony is that even that competitor belongs to the same three bureaus that sell FICO's scores. So again, the entire credit score market is one big monopoly. And that monopoly really has influence on areas of your life that are far beyond loans.

超越贷款的影响与结语

为什么这是个问题？信用评分以及它在美国和加拿大文化中根深蒂固的程度，在很多方面决定了人生的走向。最大的问题在于，它们伸手触及了像保险这样的领域。而在一个像美国这样被保险捆绑的国家（加拿大程度轻一些），它们对你的未来有着巨大的——我认为是不成比例地、毫无道理地巨大的——影响。作为一名移民，在一个你没有信用记录、也没有家人能为你担保并帮你建立信用记录的国家里开启生活，会以种种方式限制你，带来很多恐惧。我算幸运的，十几岁时就早早移民了，我用一张 500 美元额度的信用卡建立了自己的信用评分。但当时，我遇到需要贷款救命的情况的风险要低得多。而对于一个成年人来说，无论是移民还是因为某些人生境遇导致没有信用记录或信用记录很差的人，情况就完全不同了。

英文原文

Why is this a problem? Credit score and how deeply it's ingrained in American and Canadian cultures is life defining in many ways. The biggest problem is that they reach into areas like insurance. And in an insurance cuffed country like the US and to a lesser degree Canada, they have an enormous, I'd argue a disproportionately unjustifiably enormous impact on your future. As an immigrant myself, building a life in a country where you don't have a credit history and you don't have a family who can vouch for you and help you with that credit history limits you in ways that create a lot of fear. I was lucky enough to immigrate early as a teenager and I built my credit score with a $500 credit card. But back then, I was at a much lower risk of running into a situation when I needed to take a loan to save my life. For an adult, be it an immigrant or someone who had certain life circumstances that led them to either not having a credit history or having a poor one is a different story.

我给你举个例子。保险公司有一套单独的保险评分，用来预测你提出索赔的可能性。而那个评分直接决定了你的保险费用。根据法律，保险只能使用你的年龄、烟草使用情况、家庭人口数和居住地来设定保费，禁止使用信用评分。但与此同时，存在一种叫做消费者数据的东西。虽然法律规定信用评分不能用来决定你的医疗保险费用，但消费者数据却是可以合法使用的。2018 年的一项调查发现，保险公司和经纪人会根据非医疗数据来预测医疗成本，比如婚姻状况、看电视的习惯、种族，以及你是否按时支付账单。这些账单记录可以从房产记录、电话号码和社区数据中获取，这在技术上也算是一种信用评分。还有证据显示，一家公司对每个人都使用了 442 个非医疗属性的数据点来预测医疗成本。当然，没有任何证据表明某一个特定数据点会把你的保费提高多少，而且法律也不允许公司用这些来定价。但在现实中，这是一个灰色地带，而且这些数据就摆在那里。

I'll give you an example. Insurance companies have a separate insurance score that predicts how likely you are to file a claim. And that score drives the cost of your insurance. By law, insurance can only use your age, tobacco usage, family size, and location to set the premium using credit score is prohibited. But at the same time, there is such thing as consumer data. While credit score cannot be used to define the cost of your health insurance by law, consumer data is fair game. There was an investigation in 2018 that found that insurers and brokers predict health costs from non-medical data such as marital status, TV habits, race, and whether you pay your bills on time. Those bills can be taken from property records, phone numbers, and neighborhood data, which is technically a credit score of sorts. There was also evidence of how one company used 442 data points on every person of non-medical attributes to predict medical costs. Of course, there isn't any evidence that a certain data point elevates your premium by X number and the companies by law are not allowed to use that for pricing. But in reality, it is a gray area and the data is out there.
