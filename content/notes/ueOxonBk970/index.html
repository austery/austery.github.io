摘要

政治厌恶症：传统政治动员的失灵与疏离

在当下的公共生活中，政治参与正面临一种深层的“令人作呕感”——即一种让人彻底倒胃口且难以挽回的厌恶。民意调查机构皮尤研究中心（Pew Research Center: 权威跨党派社会议题与民意调查智库）的实证数据证实了这一点：当被要求用一个词来形容政治时，仅有百分之二的美国人给出了正面词汇。面对这种令人疲惫、反感甚至恐惧的政治环境，许多人选择以“数字排毒”、远离新闻或将手机锁进抽屉等自我关怀的方式回避现实。然而，彻底抽离和闭目塞听并不能阻挡政治力量对个人生活的直接干预；当我们放弃发声时，政策便不再是“为我们而定”，而是“施加于我们”。

这种集体疏离感的根源，在于长期以来被首都政治精英所垄断的传统运作体系。在华盛顿哥伦比亚特区（Washington, DC: 美国的政治中心与联邦政府所在地）的智库和咨询机构里，从业者习惯于自认为掌握了最优解，并通过自上而下的方式向大众灌输标准答案；甚至斥巨资进行海量的话语测试，迷信只要让所有人精准重复特定的话术就能赢得选举。然而，这种将真实民众抽象为备忘录里的“真实人群”而从未真正倾听的精英操作，已逐渐在基层选举与动员中暴露出巨大的无力感。

英文原文

I haven't been on the dating scene since I was 22. But I still remember “the ick.” You know, that feeling when something just completely turned you off and there [was] no coming back from it? Turns out, bad dates are not the only thing with an ick problem. Politics has it too. I'm serious. The data proves it. When researchers at Pew a few years ago asked Americans to describe politics in one word, just two percent used a positive term. Two percent. Take a moment. Really think about it. When's the last time politics made you feel something other than exhausted, disgusted, afraid? I can't remember either. And I've worked in this business for 25 years. We're all feeling the ick. So we're doing digital detoxes, news cleanses, or just putting our phones in the drawer and calling it self-care. Good stuff. But tuning out altogether, that's when things start getting done to us, not for us. And we can't afford it. Not now. So what do we do? What if we made politics something people actually want to be part of? Let me tell you what I've learned. But first, what I had to unlearn. My journey starts where all problems go to get solved. Washington, DC. (Laughter) I like to think I was different than your typical political insider, and in many ways I was. I helped start an organization whose entire job was to watch Fox News so you don't have to. (Laughter) I'm proud of that work. Thank you, it mattered. (Applause) But it was all built out of think tanks where we thought it was our job to find the right answer and ship it out to everyone else. I even helped start a spinoff devoted entirely to message testing. I truly believed [that] if we could get everyone to repeat the perfect words, we'd win. And sometimes it sort of barely worked. Even after I moved to the burbs outside Cleveland to start a family around family, I kept doing the DC thing. I was living in Ohio, but my head was in Washington.

后院里的觉醒：郊区女性与信任网络的自发重组

转折点发生在2018年美国中期选举。在通常执政党会失去约26个国会席位的规律下，当年执政党失去了43个席位，其中40个来自郊区选区，大量地方州议会席位也发生了翻转。这场转变的真正推动力量并非华盛顿的高层策划，而是全美各地过去仅参与投票、如今开始自主组织的郊区女性群体。当政治操盘手依然沉溺于特区电话会议时，变革早已在各地的后院发生。当面对家庭与公共现实的脱节，许多原本置身事外的普通女性开始走出私人空间，在葡萄酒聚会、书店和餐桌旁建立起紧密的连结。

这些基层集会展现出一种华盛顿精英永远无法人工催生的特质：高度的情感共鸣与真实的信任资本。她们并非因为原先是朋友而组织起来，而是因为共同组织而成为了深交的好友。在一个普通的后院露台上，伴随着简单的鸡尾酒与蛋饼，一位照护患有脆弱病症孩子的单亲母亲倾诉着在复杂医疗体制下的精疲力竭，另一位在保守家庭中长大的女性则坦诚表达自己对原有教条的深刻质疑。当防备彻底放下，欢笑与泪水自然流淌，“如果这就是政治，我全力以赴”的感悟便应运而生。这种基于真实同理心的本土关系网络，远比政党口号拥有更为强大的生命力。

英文原文

Everything changed for me in 2018. The 2016 presidential election had lit a fire under suburban women all over the country. Women who had never done anything more than show up to vote had started organizing. And they got results. In a typical midterm election, the party in power loses about 26 congressional seats. In 2018, they lost 43, and 40 were suburban. Even more seats flipped locally in state legislatures. Some of it was happening in my own backyard. But I had no idea. I was too busy dialing in to DC conference calls, even though I had become part of the very demographic both political parties spend millions trying to reach: a suburban mom. The morning after that election, I sat on the floor of my bedroom closet. I'd just had my second child, and for the first time, I felt completely out of alignment. Lost. I knew I couldn't be a DC operative anymore. I wanted to find those women. In DC, they search for "real people." Seriously, that's what they refer to them as in meetings and memos. But they don't really know them or even really listen to them. I wanted to. So I called up people that I knew who were working locally in Ohio politics, and I asked, “Do you know any of these suburban women who are organizing in their own neighborhoods?” It turns out they did. So I hit the road. I met some over wine, some at a bookstore, one group over dinner, and every time, I thought, these women -- I want to hang out with them. They're fun, they're fired up. And they finish each other's sentences. Each time I’d ask, “How long have you all known each other?” I figured they’d say since high school or college. "Months," they'd say. "We've known each other for months." And that's when it hit me. They didn’t organize because they were friends -- they were friends because they organized. Take Tara. She's a single mom. I was introduced to her by one of the women I'd met over wine, Stephanie. Tara was distraught over the division in our country. She wanted to do something about it. She just didn't know what yet. So I invited her over to my house along with a handful of other women. My mom made Palomas. I made her egg casserole, and we set up on my back deck. Before long, these women were opening up to each other in ways I didn't expect. One woman with a medically fragile child, who loved dinosaurs as much as my son, shared her sheer exhaustion from battling the health care system. Another, who had been raised hard-line Republican, was starting to question everything she'd been taught. There was laughter and there were tears. The kind that come when you let your guard down. And in the end, Tara said seven words I will never forget. "If this is politics, I'm all in." A few years later, Tara ran for school board and won. But this story is not about just one woman or one backyard. I started meeting women like Tara all over Ohio, all over the country. Women who'd never done anything political before were getting together and getting organized. And all of them had something DC can never get. Real networks, real trust. But none of them knew about each other. I wanted to connect them and help them tap into the power they were just beginning to realize they had.

关系型动员的力量：超越竞选周期的社区共治

为了将这些分散的草根力量系统化聚合，草根倡导组织红酒与蓝（Red, Wine and Blue: 致力于通过社交网络和日常生活场景赋能郊区女性参与公民行动的非营利组织）成立了，其核心准则便是坚决摒弃任何充斥官僚与政党刻板气息的话语体系。与传统政治竞选中让志愿者敲击陌生人房门、拨打冷电呼叫的冰冷模式截然相反，这一模式转向了关系型动员（Relational Organizing: 利用个体既有的亲友圈、同事圈与邻里人际网进行公民动员的去中心化策略）。组织成员绘制属于自己的关系网络图谱，将沟通重点放在身边的朋友、邻居、读书会成员以及校门口接送孩子的同伴身上，由熟人带动熟人，实现责任与选民意愿的层层扩散。

为了化解日常交往中谈论公共议题的尴尬与恐惧，社群通过欢乐时光、知识竞答或“禁书宾果”等轻松的社群活动共同学习。在这些活动中，成员们彼此熟悉、共同演练，从而培育出传统政治机器无法批量生产的关键资产——并非精雕细琢的话术，而是面对棘手对话的勇气与底气。数据分析显示了惊人的实战效能：传统竞选活动花费数百万美元往往只能让投票率微升零点几个百分点，而基于熟人网络的面对面沟通能使目标受众的投票率直接跃升5到7个百分点。

更为重要的是，真正的友谊不会随着选举日落幕而消散。在选举周期之后，这些跨越党派立场的女性社群持续在本地深耕：她们直面移民执法机构的争议、审查人工智能数据中心建设对本地资源的消耗，并在学校董事会中反对审查与下架图书。这证明了政治的答案不在遥远的政治中枢，而存在于普通人聚首交流的客厅与后院。当人们不再被迫在“守护公共世界”与“关照个体生活”之间做割裂的二选一时，政治便重新演化为一种能带来连接感、尊严与实效的共同体实践。

英文原文

So I hired two of the women from my road trip, and we got to work. Our first rule: if anything we did or said sounded like a political party, we'd throw it out and start over. Starting with the name. We called it "Red, Wine and Blue." The wine is optional. Diet Coke, Spindrift, Palomas and egg casserole, whatever. But how things got done needed an overhaul too. Traditional campaigns have their volunteers knock on strangers' doors and call people they've never met. We do the opposite. You see, every single one of us here already have a powerful network: friends, neighbors, coworkers, people from your book club, the school pick-up line. So we start there. We map out everyone we know who could be with us, and we take responsibility for making sure they vote. Then those people talk to their people and on and on. Sounds simple, but you don't want to make things awkward. What do you say to the friend who says, “I don’t do politics?” Or the neighbor who says, “Voting doesn’t matter?” This is where the friendship part comes in. We have happy hours or unhappy hours, depending on the day. We do trivia nights, banned book bingo -- we learn together and get to know each other as people. So when it comes time to have those hard conversations, we know we're not doing it alone. We've practiced, we've gotten brave together. And that's the thing no political campaign can manufacture. Not the message -- the nerve. That alone would be worth it. But you know what makes it even better? It works. When we hired an independent analyst to run the numbers, they ran them. Then they ran them again. They thought there had to be a mistake. I thought there had to be a mistake. Because here's what they showed. When our members reach out to the people they know -- not strangers, the people they know -- voter turnout among the people they contact goes up five to seven percentage points. Now listen, campaigns spend millions to move that number by a fraction of a point. A conversation between friends moves it by five. Now here's the best part. Friendship doesn’t go away after Election Day. So those same women are working together right now in their communities to stand up to ICE, take on AI data centers and school boards trying to ban books and take down pride flags. And they come from every side of the aisle. So if you're sitting in this room right now thinking that sounds appealing -- but also overwhelming -- I am talking to you. I know it's a hard moment to feel hopeful. I feel it too. But I promise you, no matter where you live, there are people who feel like you do right around the corner. And if you've never done anything political before, you are exactly who this moment needs most. Because the answers are not in DC -- they are in living rooms and backyards among people who are getting together and making their communities the kinds of places they want to live in. And when we do it together, as friends who have each other's backs and show up for each other even when it's hard, that's what makes politics feel good. And that's something people want to be part of and something that actually works for all of us. So find your people, get together, start talking about what matters to you. Because as it turns out, we don't have to choose between taking care of our world and taking care of ourselves if we just do it together. Thank you. (Applause)
